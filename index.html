research statement

Interpretability and VLMs

My interest in interpretability research started, like it does for a lot of people, from how interesting it is to look inside a neural network at all. It became more concrete after reading Chris Olah's work on interpretability. One post on manifolds and topology in particular gave me a way to think about what a network is actually doing. A network progressively transforms the geometry of the data, so that structure which is hard to separate in the input becomes easy to separate later. Learning is not only about adding neurons or dimensions, it is about transforming the representation itself. That idea, along with the work on feature visualisation and circuits in Distill, made me want to treat networks as systems whose internals can be studied, and not only as models that produce useful outputs.

On the other hand, I was also working with VLMs during my research in the final years of my undergraduate degree. I compared three fusion architectures for Arabic hateful meme detection, and then in my MediaEval submission and my MICCAI 2026 workshop paper I looked at whether a richer training signal helps, by supervising the model on where to look rather than only on the final answer. Those projects showed me that there are many ways to improve a VLM: change the architecture, change how it is trained, or change what it learns from. Eventually that led me to a different question. Instead of modifying the architecture or the objective again, can interpretability tell us what these models build inside, and point to better ways of training small ones?

Research direction moving forward

This is where my current interest lies, at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and vision-language models.

A recent benchmark, VRIQ, caught my attention because it looked at where current VLMs fail on visual reasoning. A large share of the failures turned out to be perception errors rather than reasoning errors. The model fails what looks like a reasoning task because it read something in the image wrongly at an earlier stage.

That connects to the ICLR 2025 paper by Neo et al., which studied how visual information is represented and processed across the layers of a VLM. They found that visual tokens contribute substantially to the prediction, and that their representations become more aligned with textual concepts deeper in the network.

So rather than asking only whether a model answers a visual question correctly, I want to ask a more mechanistic question: at what point in the network does the information needed for a perceptual decision become strong enough to determine the answer?

I see this as a bridge between my earlier work and the interpretability literature. Until now I have approached VLMs by asking how to change the architecture or the training to improve performance. I now want to start from the other end, by asking what representations and computations are already emerging inside the model, and how they relate to particular capabilities.

This also connects to a longer-term question I want to explore, which is whether these internal representations can explain the gap between large and small VLMs. A small model may not simply have less capacity or less knowledge. It may build a different internal representation for the same capability, and that difference is invisible if you only compare outputs.

If I can identify the representations that matter for a capability in a larger model, and understand how they differ in a smaller one, that could give a more informed basis for compression or knowledge distillation. I do not treat this as a fixed hypothesis, and I am not claiming that interpretability will necessarily produce a better distillation method. It is the direction I want to investigate: first understand the mechanism behind a capability, then see whether that understanding helps transfer it. Recent work on how distillation changes internal circuits suggests that a student can reorganise its computation rather than copy the teacher's, which is part of why the question interests me.

The experiment

As a first step I wanted to study perception in a setting where the visual information can be controlled exactly. I replicated the analysis from the ICLR paper on a more recent 3B vision-language model from Liquid AI, and then designed a new experiment that uses activation patching on synthetic scenes to isolate one perceptual ability at a time. The setup is described in chapter 05 of the timeline, and the full write-up with every result is here.

In the initial results the categories did not behave the same way. For counting and relation the original answer could be recovered by patching in the early-to-middle layers, around layers 5 to 13, after which patching had little effect. For the other categories the same effect appeared later, around layer 21 and beyond. I am still working through what this means.

I see this as a modest first step rather than an answer. What it gives me is a concrete way to ask whether different perceptual abilities are built through different internal representations.

Conclusion

My longer-term interest is in understanding what gives models their capabilities at the level of representation and computation, particularly in vision-language models. I started from questions about architectures and training, and that has gradually led me to questions about what the model has actually learned inside.

What I care about most is approaching this incrementally. The broader question is ambitious, but meaningful research can start from smaller controlled questions.
